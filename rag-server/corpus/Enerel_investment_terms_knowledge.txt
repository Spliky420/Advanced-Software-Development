Exchange-traded fund (ETF): An exchange-traded fund, or ETF, is an investment fund whose units trade on a stock exchange, such as the ASX, throughout the trading day like ordinary shares. Most ETFs are passive and aim to track an index, such as the S&P/ASX 200, by holding the same securities as the index. ETFs give investors diversification in a single trade, usually charge lower fees than active funds, and publish their holdings regularly so investors can see what they own.

ETF pricing and net asset value: An ETF unit's market price is set by buyers and sellers on the exchange, while its net asset value, or NAV, is the value of the fund's underlying holdings divided by the number of units. Market makers create and redeem units to keep the market price close to the NAV. When an ETF's price is above its NAV it trades at a premium, and when below it trades at a discount to its holdings.

Index fund: An index fund is a fund that aims to match the return of a market index rather than beat it, by holding all or a representative sample of the securities in that index. Index funds can be unlisted managed funds or exchange-traded funds. Because they do not pay analysts to pick stocks, index funds usually have lower fees than actively managed funds. Their actual return differs slightly from the index because of fees, cash holdings and tracking error.

Tracking error: Tracking error measures how closely an index fund or ETF follows the index it is designed to track. It is usually expressed as the standard deviation of the difference between the fund's returns and the index's returns over a period. A low tracking error means the fund follows its index closely. Tracking error is caused by management fees, transaction costs, cash held for redemptions, dividend timing, and sampling, where the fund holds only some of the index's securities.

Management expense ratio (MER): The management expense ratio, also called the management fee, is the annual cost of running a fund expressed as a percentage of the fund's assets. It is deducted from the fund's assets rather than charged as a separate bill, so it reduces the fund's returns. Broad index ETFs often charge well under half a percent per year, while actively managed funds usually charge more. Small fee differences compound into large differences in wealth over many decades.

Bid-ask spread: The bid-ask spread is the difference between the highest price a buyer is willing to pay for a security, the bid, and the lowest price a seller will accept, the ask. It is a hidden trading cost paid each time an investor buys or sells. Large and heavily traded ETFs and shares usually have narrow spreads, while thinly traded ones can have wide spreads. Using limit orders helps investors avoid paying an unexpectedly wide spread when they trade.

Managed investment scheme: A managed investment scheme, often called a managed fund, pools money from many investors so that a professional manager can invest it on their behalf in assets such as shares, bonds or property. In Australia, schemes offered to retail investors must generally be registered with ASIC under the Corporations Act and operated by a responsible entity. Investors own units in the scheme rather than the underlying assets. ETFs and unit trusts are types of managed investment scheme.

Responsible entity: A responsible entity is the public company that operates a registered managed investment scheme in Australia. It must hold an Australian financial services licence and owes duties to scheme members under the Corporations Act, including acting honestly, exercising care and diligence, and acting in the best interests of members. The responsible entity must have a compliance plan and a constitution for the scheme. ASIC regulatory guides explain how ASIC expects responsible entities to meet these obligations in practice.

Diversification: Diversification means spreading investments across many different individual securities, asset classes, industries and countries so that a loss in one investment has a smaller effect on the whole portfolio. It reduces the risk that comes from any single company or sector, called specific risk, but it cannot remove market risk, which affects almost all investments at once. A single broad index ETF can hold hundreds of companies, which is why ETFs are a simple way to diversify a portfolio.

Asset allocation and rebalancing: Asset allocation is how a portfolio is divided between asset classes such as Australian shares, international shares, bonds, property and cash. It is usually chosen to match an investor's goals, time horizon and tolerance for risk, and it drives most of the variation in a portfolio's returns. Market movements shift a portfolio away from its target mix, so investors rebalance by selling assets above target and buying assets below it, restoring the portfolio's intended risk level.

Dollar-cost averaging: Dollar-cost averaging, or DCA, is an investment strategy in which an investor invests a fixed amount at regular intervals, such as every month, regardless of the price. When prices are low the fixed amount buys more units, and when prices are high it buys fewer. DCA reduces the risk of investing a large sum just before a market fall and builds a regular saving habit. It does not guarantee a profit or prevent losses in a falling market.

Market capitalisation: Market capitalisation, or market cap, is the total stock market value of a company's shares on issue, calculated by multiplying the share price by the number of shares outstanding. Companies are often grouped as large cap, mid cap or small cap by this measure. Most share market indices, such as the S&P/ASX 200, are weighted by market capitalisation, so the very largest companies make up the biggest share of the index and of any ETF that tracks it.

Price-to-earnings ratio (P/E ratio): The price-to-earnings ratio, or P/E ratio, compares a company's share price with its earnings per share. A forward P/E uses forecast earnings for the next year, while a trailing P/E uses earnings from the past year. A high P/E can mean investors expect strong growth, or that the shares are expensive; a low P/E can mean the opposite. Analysts compare P/E ratios with a market's long-term averages and similar companies to judge whether valuations look stretched.

Dividend and dividend yield: A dividend is a share of a company's profit paid to its shareholders, usually in cash and often twice a year in Australia. ETFs pass the dividends of the companies they hold on to unitholders as distributions. Dividend yield is the annual dividend per share divided by the share price, expressed as a percentage. A high yield can be attractive for income, but it can also signal a falling share price or an unsustainable dividend payment.

Franking credits: Franking credits, also called imputation credits, are tax credits attached to dividends paid by Australian companies out of profits that have already been taxed at the company tax rate. Shareholders include the credit in their taxable income and use it to reduce the tax they owe, so the same profit is not taxed twice. Eligible investors whose credits exceed their tax liability can receive a refund. Australian share ETFs pass franking credits through to their unitholders with distributions.

Capital gain and capital gains tax: A capital gain is the profit made when an asset such as a share or ETF unit is sold for more than it cost, and a capital loss arises when it is sold for less. In Australia capital gains are added to taxable income. Individuals who hold an asset for at least twelve months can usually reduce the taxable gain by a fifty percent discount. Capital losses can offset capital gains but not wages.

Volatility: Volatility describes how much and how quickly the price of an investment moves up and down. It is commonly measured as the standard deviation of returns over a chosen period. Shares are generally much more volatile than bonds, and bonds more volatile than cash. High volatility means a wider range of possible outcomes over short periods, so volatile investments suit investors with long time horizons who can wait out temporary falls without being forced to sell at a loss.

Liquidity: Liquidity is how easily and quickly an investment can be converted into cash without significantly affecting its price. Cash and large listed shares and ETFs are highly liquid, because they can be sold on an exchange within seconds at close to their quoted price. Property, unlisted funds and some bonds are less liquid and may take weeks to sell or require a discount. An ETF's liquidity depends mainly on the liquidity of its underlying holdings, not just its volume.

Cash rate: The cash rate is the interest rate on overnight loans between banks, and in Australia the Reserve Bank of Australia, or RBA, sets a target for it at its monetary policy meetings. Changes in the cash rate flow through to variable mortgage rates, business loans, savings accounts and term deposits. The RBA raises the cash rate to slow inflation and lowers it to support activity and employment, aiming to keep inflation within two to three percent on average.

Inflation and trimmed-mean inflation: Inflation is the rate at which the general level of prices rises, which reduces what a dollar can buy. In Australia it is measured by the Consumer Price Index, or CPI, published by the Australian Bureau of Statistics. Trimmed-mean inflation is an underlying measure that removes the items with the largest and smallest price changes each period, so one-off price shocks do not distort the trend. The RBA watches it closely when setting the cash rate.

Bonds, coupons and yield: A bond is a loan from an investor to a government or company that pays regular interest, called coupons, and repays the face value at maturity. Bond prices move in the opposite direction to interest rates: when market rates rise, existing bonds generally fall in price, and when rates fall they rise. A bond's yield to maturity is the total annual return expected if it is held to maturity, combining its coupons with any price change.

Duration and interest rate risk: Duration measures how sensitive a bond's price is to changes in interest rates, expressed in years. A bond with a duration of five years would fall roughly five percent in price if interest rates rose by one percentage point. Long-duration bonds are more sensitive to rate changes than short-duration bonds. Convexity describes how duration itself changes as rates move, so bonds with higher convexity gain more when rates fall than they lose when rates rise.

Barbell, bullet and ladder bond strategies: A barbell strategy holds short-term and long-term bonds while avoiding intermediate maturities, combining the liquidity of short bonds with the price gains long bonds deliver if rates fall. A bullet strategy concentrates bonds around one target maturity date. A bond ladder spreads holdings evenly across many maturities, so a bond matures regularly and is reinvested at current rates. Barbells need more active rebalancing than a ladder and carry reinvestment risk at the short end.

Term deposit: A term deposit is a bank account in which money is locked for a fixed period, from one month to several years, at a fixed interest rate. Withdrawing early usually means a penalty or reduced interest. Term deposit rates generally follow the cash rate, rising when the RBA raises rates. In Australia deposits with an authorised deposit-taking institution are protected by the Financial Claims Scheme up to two hundred and fifty thousand dollars per account holder per institution.

Behavioural biases in investing: Behavioural biases are predictable thinking errors that lead investors to make poor decisions. Loss aversion makes losses feel about twice as painful as equal gains feel good. Confirmation bias makes investors favour information that supports what they already believe. Recency bias overweights recent events. Herding means following the crowd into popular trades. Overconfidence leads investors to trade too often. A written investment plan reviewed on a fixed schedule helps investors avoid reacting emotionally to market moves.
